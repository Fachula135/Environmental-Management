What is this project about?

In 2015 Pope Francis wrote Laudato Si', a letter about caring for our common home. It is written for everyone, not only Catholics.

Our questionCan technology and markets alone fix environmental problems?Pollution, waste and climate change are often taught as technical problems. We wanted to test that idea.
Our hypothesisNo.Lasting solutions also need us to face global inequality and to replace the throwaway culture with a circular economy that looks after people and nature.

Four ideas that explain everything

Tap a card to find out what it means. Then try the activities below.

We take, make, use and throw away. Nature works in a circle where waste becomes food for something else. Our economy works in a line. And sometimes we throw away people too, like workers and communities who are treated as if they do not matter.
Everything is closely connected: the climate, animals and plants, people, and justice. You cannot fix one without looking at the others.
Wealthier countries use far more resources and release far more pollution. Because of that, Pope Francis says they owe a debt to poorer countries, which suffer the damage but caused less of it.
The book of Genesis says humans should "till and keep" the Earth. Pope Francis explains that "dominion" does not mean using nature however we like. It means looking after it, like a gardener.

Three real cases

We acted like environmental detectives. We used published data and read each case through the four ideas.

400+coastal dead zones

Scientists have documented more than 400 "dead zones" along the world's coasts, where the water has too little oxygen for sea life. Fertilizer runoff from farms is a leading cause (Diaz and Rosenberg, 2008).

    Producers keep the profit. Who pays for the damage downstream?

    Phones, laptops and appliances become e-waste. Look how much there is, and how little is documented as recycled (Forti et al., 2020; Baldé et al., 2024).

    Much of the rest is dumped, burned or processed informally, often by workers in lower-income countries who are exposed to toxic substances. Recycling technology mostly exists. What is missing are the rules that make it a must.

    What do you do with your old phone?

    3.3 to 3.6billion people

    This is how many people live in places that are highly vulnerable to climate change (IPCC, 2022). That means communities with poverty, weak infrastructure and little ability to adapt.

    100million more people in poverty by 2030

    That is the World Bank's estimate if there is no action (Hallegatte et al., 2016). These communities contributed the least to historical emissions. This is the ecological debt in real life, and it is what Pope Francis means by hearing both the cry of the Earth and the cry of the poor.

    What the three cases have in common

    Open each pattern to see it in every case.

    Is technology enough?

    Not on its own. It is necessary, but it is not sufficient.

    What technology can do

    Precision farming wastes less fertilizer. Better recycling recovers materials. Low-carbon energy cuts emissions.

    What it cannot do alone

    It does not decide who bears the remaining harm, it does not force companies to design products for reuse, and it does not settle who pays for adaptation. Markets also do not count damage unless rules make them.

    What can be done?

    Match responsibility to contribution

    Climate finance for vulnerable countries. Extended producer responsibility, which means companies are responsible for electronics and packaging after people stop using them.

    Count the costs they now pass on

    Add the damage to rivers, workers and communities to the real cost of a product. Design things to be repaired and reused, not thrown away.

    Start at the scale you can reach

    Our essay suggests studying a local case. For example, how a circular practice changes both the amount of waste and who suffers the harm in one community. Choose your own first step:

    The cry of the Earth and the cry of the poor are one cry.

    Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015), paragraph 49
